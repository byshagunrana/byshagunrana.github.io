09 · Consumer insight / Luxury beauty

A memory, or a scent note?

Testing two ways to begin fragrance discovery.

I wanted to know if beginning with a memory made exploring fragrance feel more personal. Forty adults gave me an answer I had not expected: the route that felt more personal was not the one that felt easier.

01 / Why I tested it

I had liked the idea. I wanted to see what people thought.

For L’Oréal Brandstorm 2026, my three-person team explored a digital fragrance-discovery idea centred on scent memory. A memory seemed like a more intuitive beginning for someone who might know the feeling they wanted, but not how to describe it using fragrance terminology.

After the competition, that assumption stayed with me. Would a memory-led beginning really help someone discover fragrance, or would a recognizable scent family make choosing easier?

So I designed a small independent comparison using two Google Forms. This study happened later, outside Brandstorm. It was not commissioned by L’Oréal, included in its judging, or conducted with any fragrance Maison.

02 / Study design

Change the beginning.
Keep the choice the same.

Each participant completed one version of an anonymous questionnaire. The opening prompt differed; the fictional fragrance profiles and seven follow-up questions matched.

Both forms showed the same three written, fictional fragrance profiles in the same order. Participants chose which profile they would sample first and rated interest, ease, confidence and personal relevance on 1–5 scales. They also reported prior fragrance-buying familiarity and one purchase-decision consideration.

The procedure used to assign participants to the two forms was not documented. This is a descriptive comparison between two groups, not a randomized experiment.

06 / Study in one frame
07 / What I'd do differently

Don't make customers choose between meaning and clarity.

The more useful next question isn't “Which prompt wins?” It is whether we could retain an emotional starting point without making the next decision harder.

AMemory-first

An optional memory prompt, followed by the same scent descriptions.

BNotes-first

A scent-family prompt followed by the same scent descriptions.

CHybrid

A skippable memory prompt, then an optional, editable scent-family filter before sampling.

And add an actual sense of smell.

A second study would record group assignment or randomize it, recruit a larger and more varied adult sample, vary fragrance presentation order, and include real samples where possible. The primary outcome should be chosen before collecting responses.

08 / Research boundaries

What this study doesn't prove.

Original data deserves an honest interpretation, especially when the result is tempting to turn into a headline.

  • Small, voluntary sample. Forty distinct adults, not a representative sample of luxury fragrance buyers or a named demographic.
  • No documented allocation method. We cannot describe the groups as randomized or attribute their differences to the prompts.
  • Text descriptions, not real perfumes. No physical smelling, purchases, conversion tracking or delayed behavioural outcomes.
  • Immediate self-report and fixed profile order. Responses capture stated first impressions, not verified choices or long-term effects.
  • Different prior familiarity. Existing fragrance experience may partly explain group differences; no causal effect was isolated.
