Project 08 · Competition case study

What if fragrance discovery began with a memory?

A scent-memory concept for L’Oréal Brandstorm 2026. Our three-person team explored how a personal memory might help a young fragrance customer begin discovering a scent, before confronting the real question: would it lead to a meaningful trial?

01 · The brief and the question

Fragrance is invisible. How do you make discovery feel personal?

The official 2026 challenge, Craft the Future of Luxury Fragrance, asked participants to imagine new products and experiences for luxury perfumery with L’Oréal Luxe. Our team chose to think about the beginning of discovery rather than inventing another bottle or simply making a product recommendation interface.

Fragrance shopping often begins with notes, brands, familiar favourites or a salesperson's questions. We wondered whether asking about an occasion, place or feeling could give someone who does not yet know fragrance vocabulary a more intuitive place to start.

This was a concept hypothesis, not a claim that memories reliably predict olfactory preferences.

02 · Research and framing

We looked at the fragrance landscape before deciding what to propose.

Our team reviewed 25 fragrance portfolios. That work gave us a comparative starting point for discussing brand worlds, how fragrance is presented, and the distance between product language and a customer's emotional starting point.

01Look across portfolios

Compare how luxury fragrances are named, described and presented; identify different approaches to communicating an intangible product.

02Question the entry point

Ask whether familiarity with notes, houses or categories is a prerequisite for discovery, especially for a younger customer exploring fragrance.

03Develop a concept direction

Explore a memory-led digital entry point that connects feeling to scent exploration without claiming perfect personalisation.

04Tell a clear story

Condense the thinking into the competition's short slide proposal and team presentation video.

This is a retrospective account of our approach, not a reproduced dataset or a claim that the 25-portfolio review established consumer demand. The original underlying comparison is not published here.

03 · The concept

Start with what a fragrance might bring back.

Instead of opening with a scent pyramid or a named perfume, the proposed experience would invite a person to describe a memory, atmosphere or emotional association. That becomes a conversation starter for discovering fragrances, not a diagnostic test.

01Memory

A place, encounter, season or moment

02Mood

The feeling connected to it

03Scent family

A tentative sensory direction

04Fragrance world

A few relevant expressions to explore

05Trial

Smell, compare and decide for yourself

This five-step path is an editorial reconstruction of the concept for this portfolio. It is not presented as a tested algorithm or a verbatim feature list from the competition submission.

04 · The decisions behind it

Emotion can open the door. It cannot replace the nose.

The idea became more useful when we distinguished an engaging discovery prompt from the actual job of choosing a fragrance.

01
Make the first question easy to answer

A person may not know what amber, iris or aldehydic means. They may know what a particular evening felt like.

02
Do not confuse association with accuracy

Two people can remember the same place differently and dislike the same fragrance. Suggestions would need to remain provisional.

03
Keep discovery voluntary

Memories can be private. A prompt should be skippable, with no unnecessary personal history collected or stored.

04
End with real smelling

The successful handoff would be from a story to a physical sample or consultation, not merely to another screen.

These design principles are a later strategic articulation of what the concept would need to work responsibly. They should not be read as features implemented or validated during the challenge.

06 · What we would need to find out

The concept was submitted. The next proof would have to come from people.

The competition format required a concise idea, not evidence of a live consumer product. A real pilot would need to test whether this entry point helps discovery rather than just making a beautiful story.

Can people use it without effort?Prompt comprehension, skip rate and completion
Does it widen useful discovery?Relevance of suggested scent families and confidence to try
Does it lead to a genuine trial?Sample acceptance, consultation interest and comparative smelling
Does it respect the customer?Optional disclosure, consent and no pressure to narrate a private memory

07 · Submission and outcome

2026 / L’ORÉAL BRANDSTORM

Certificate of Recognition.

Our team submitted a three-slide concept and a three-minute video for the L’Oréal Luxe fragrance brief. I received a Certificate of Recognition for participation. The team was not a competition winner.

Working on the idea pushed me to ask whether an evocative concept could survive a much less romantic question: would it actually help someone choose what to smell?
What happened after Brandstorm / October 2026

I tested the question with 40 adults.

In a separate, original research study, I compared a memory-first opening against a scent-family opening using the same three fictional fragrance descriptions. The memory-first version received higher personal-relevance ratings; notes-first was rated easier to choose from. It challenged the assumption behind the competition concept without claiming a causal effect.
